# Automated Early Leaderboard Generation (Singh et al. 2018/2019) and Its Lineage Through 2026

## The paper itself: table parsing, entity normalization, graph construction, ranking algorithms, evaluation, limitations

### Takeaway
Singh, Sarkar, Vyas, Goyal, Mukherjee & Chakrabarti, "Automated Early Leaderboard Generation From Comparative Tables" (arXiv:1802.04538, ECIR 2019), build leaderboards by parsing LaTeX tables from arXiv CS papers into a "performance improvement graph" of noisy pairwise comparisons, then run tournament-ranking algorithms over that graph — and find that PageRank-style aggregation beats sports-style tournament methods (linear/exponential tournament, sink-counting, cocitation) because the comparison graph is sparse, disconnected, and structurally biased toward papers with zero outgoing edges (sinks).

### Cited Findings
- Authors: Mayank Singh, Rajdeep Sarkar, Atharva Vyas, Pawan Goyal, Animesh Mukherjee, Soumen Chakrabarti; published at ECIR 2019; arXiv:1802.04538 (v2). — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.04538)
- The system "automatically discover[s] and maintain[s] leaderboards in the form of partial orders between papers, based on performance reported therein," extracting from "relatively cleaner, structured parts of the papers, e.g., performance tables" rather than full text. — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.04538)
- **Table parsing pipeline**: reference extraction → reference mapping → table extraction → metric identification → edge orientation, using "simple heuristic rules" (e.g., prefer higher F1, prefer lower execution time) to decide which of two compared systems "wins" on a given metric. — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1802.04538)
- **Entity/metric identification**: performance comparisons come from "table citations" — rows in a table where each row cites another paper as the competing technique, and column headers are the metric names. The paper explicitly notes "there is no control on the metric names, as they come from an open vocabulary" — headers are arbitrary free-text strings, i.e. metric normalization is an open, largely unsolved problem in this system rather than a solved taxonomy match. — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1802.04538)
- **Performance improvement graph**: each extracted table-citation comparison becomes a directed edge between two paper-nodes, annotated with a tuple of (metric z, the two performance values v1/v2, the source paper P asserting the comparison) and oriented from loser to winner (or winner to loser) per the metric-direction heuristics. — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1802.04538)
- **Graph sanitization, two stages**: (1) *local sanitization* — multi-edges between the same paper pair are consolidated via an unweighted graph, a weighted-count graph, or a sigmoid-weighted graph; (2) *global aggregation* — a graph-wide ranking algorithm is run over the consolidated graph to produce the final partial order/ranking. — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1802.04538)
- **Outlier/noise control**: edges are pruned when the Relative Error Improvement (REI) = (v_z − u_z)/u_z exceeds 100%; in validated leaderboards "less than 0.5% of edges have REI above 100%," while erroneous extractions show REI far above that, so the 100% REI cutoff is used as an extraction-error filter. — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1802.04538)
- **Ranking algorithms compared (6 families)**: (1) Sink nodes — papers with zero out-edges taken as local maxima/best; (2) Cocitation — count of subsequent papers that compare against a given paper; (3) Linear tournament — sports-ranking-style matrix-power method, lim_{T→∞} Σ M̄^t d̄; (4) Exponential tournament — maximum-likelihood estimate of win probabilities fit via gradient descent (this is effectively a Bradley-Terry-style approach, though the paper frames it as "tournament" ranking rather than citing BT explicitly in the fetched summary); (5) PageRank variants — weighted PageRank over the aggregated graph with damping factor α = 0.90, run over each of the three sanitization weightings (unweighted / count-weighted / sigmoid-weighted). — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1802.04538)
- **Best performer**: "PageRank variations performed significantly better than tournament variations." The sigmoid-weighted PageRank configuration achieved roughly double the recall and NDCG of citation-based baselines: Recall@10 = 19.35% and NDCG@10 = 0.154 for the best sigmoid-PageRank config, versus Recall@10 = 7.38% for a Google-Scholar-citation-count baseline (Semantic Scholar also compared, underperforming PageRank). — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1802.04538)
- **Why tournament methods failed**: the paper attributes this to structural properties of the real comparison graph that violate tournament-method assumptions — "disconnected components is a common characteristic," pairs of papers have unequal numbers of comparisons between them, and tournament algorithms are biased toward zero-out-degree (sink) nodes, such that "more than half of the tournament based top-ranked papers [were] sink nodes" (i.e., papers nobody had yet compared against, not necessarily strong papers). — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1802.04538)
- **Evaluation corpus**: 107,795 CS papers from arXiv (1993–2017) with LaTeX source, yielding 204,264 tables, 98,943 table citations, and 14,947 distinct/unique metric strings — the scale of that unique-metric count is itself evidence of the metric-name normalization problem (14,947 surface forms for presumably far fewer underlying metrics). — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1802.04538)
- **Gold leaderboards used for evaluation**: 4 manually curated leaderboards — SQuAD (117 papers), Cityscapes, PASCAL VOC, MIT-300 (saliency) — plus 27 popular "state of the art" deep-learning topics sourced from a GitHub list with 2,600+ stargazers, used as a broader validation set across CS areas. — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1802.04538)
- **Evaluation metrics used**: Recall@10, Recall@20, NDCG@10, NDCG@20, and Spearman's rank correlation against the curated/gold leaderboard orderings. — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1802.04538)
- **Per-leaderboard correlation results**: SQuAD showed the strongest agreement with the gold ranking (Spearman correlation 0.94 on F1, 0.89 on Exact Match); Cityscapes and PASCAL VOC also showed strong correlation (0.7 and 0.57 respectively); MIT-300 (saliency) showed weaker correlation (0.23 on AUC, 0.53 on SIM), which the paper attributes to disconnected components in that leaderboard's comparison graph. — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1802.04538)
- **Stated limitations**: (1) table-level reading cannot substitute for expert judgment about differing training protocols/hyperparameters/dataset variants behind a number; (2) metric-name variability across papers complicates cross-paper normalization; (3) the system does not handle multi-objective tradeoffs (e.g., precision vs. recall, time vs. space) in a principled way; (4) disconnected graph components degrade ranking reliability (directly demonstrated by the weak MIT-300 result); (5) the pipeline is limited to LaTeX source tables — figures/charts are not exploited as a results source; (6) the paper does not establish system accuracy specifically on papers reporting under identical dataset/experimental settings (a scope gap it flags rather than resolves). — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1802.04538)

### Inferences
- The paper's central methodological lesson for any downstream "normalize heterogeneous benchmark results into comparable evidence" system: **treat cross-paper performance comparisons as a noisy, incomplete tournament, not as a clean directed acyclic graph** — and prefer diffusion/centrality-style aggregation (PageRank) over win/loss tournament-style aggregation (Elo/Bradley-Terry-like MLE, linear tournament) specifically *because* real-world evidence graphs are sparse and disconnected with skewed comparison counts. This is a direct, citable precedent against naively using a simple win-rate or Elo ladder over incomplete evidence.
- The REI (relative error improvement) 100% cutoff is a cheap, reusable noise-filtering heuristic: any evidence claiming a >2x relative improvement on the same metric/dataset pair is disproportionately likely to be an extraction error (misaligned units, wrong metric direction, or table-parsing failure) rather than a real result — a useful sanity check for a system ingesting reported metrics from many procedures/sources.
- The open-vocabulary metric-name problem (14,947 unique strings for evaluation metrics) at 2018 scale is the same problem later systems (TDMS-IE, AxCell, LEGOBench, SciLead) spend most of their engineering effort on — it was not solved by Singh et al. 2019, only sanitized around via graph statistics.

### Gaps
- The specific mathematical form of the "exponential tournament" MLE step (whether it is formally identical to Bradley-Terry, or a distinct formulation) was not recoverable from the fetched ar5iv rendering; the summary tool paraphrased it as "maximum likelihood estimation of win probabilities via gradient descent" without giving the loss function.
- Exact precision numbers (not just recall/NDCG) and the full per-algorithm table (all 6 methods × all evaluation sets) were not extracted verbatim — only headline comparisons (PageRank vs. tournament vs. citation baselines) were retrievable from the fetched content.

## Follow-up work: TDMS-IE, AxCell, SciREX, ORKG-Leaderboards, LLM-based extraction (2023–2026), and Papers with Code's 2025 shutdown

### Takeaway
The lineage after Singh et al. 2019 moves from graph/heuristic table-comparison methods toward supervised information extraction of explicit (Task, Dataset, Metric, Score) — "TDMS" — tuples: Hou et al. 2019 (TDMS-IE, ACL) defined the TDMS extraction task and dataset; Kardas et al. 2020 (AxCell, EMNLP) built a closed-domain, taxonomy-linked pipeline against the Papers with Code (PWC) taxonomy and roughly tripled full-tuple extraction F1 over TDMS-IE; subsequent transformer-based systems (Kabongo/D'Souza/Auer 2021) pushed component-level F1 above 90%; by 2023–2026 the field shifted to LLM-based extraction and benchmarking (LEGOBench 2024, SciLead/UKPLab 2024, League/LAG 2025, MetaLead 2026), while Papers with Code — the taxonomy and gold-standard data source nearly every one of these systems depended on — shut down in July 2025, leaving only an archived snapshot (Hugging Face `pwc-archive`) and prompting a partial handoff to Hugging Face's "Trending Papers" (which does not replace the task-by-task leaderboard structure). ORKG-Leaderboards offers a parallel, ongoing human-in-the-loop knowledge-graph alternative.

### Cited Findings
- **TDMS-IE** (Hou, Jochim, Gleize, Bonin, Ganguly, "Identification of Tasks, Datasets, Evaluation Metrics, and Numeric Scores for Scientific Leaderboards Construction," ACL 2019, submitted June 21 2019, arXiv:1906.09317): frames the task explicitly as extracting (task, dataset, metric, score) — TDMS — tuples from NLP papers "towards the automatic construction of leaderboards," and reports the model "outperforms several baselines by a large margin." — [arXiv:1906.09317](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.09317), [ACL Anthology P19-1513](https://aclanthology.org/P19-1513/)
- TDMS-IE numeric results (from search-indexed secondary summary, not directly verified against the PDF in this session): macro-F1 65.3 / micro-F1 67.8 for TDM (task/dataset/metric) triple extraction; macro-F1 56.6 / micro-F1 66.0 when restricted to papers with at least one non-"Unknown" TDM annotation; full TDMS (including numeric score) extraction dropped sharply to macro-F1 9.9 / micro-F1 11.8, showing that attaching the correct *score* to a correctly identified (task, dataset, metric) triple is far harder than identifying the triple itself. — [search-summarized, unverified against primary PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.09317)
- **AxCell** (Kardas, Czapla, Stenetorp, Ruder, Riedel, Taylor, Stojnic, "AxCell: Automatic Extraction of Results from Machine Learning Papers," EMNLP 2020, pp. 8580–8594; arXiv:2004.14356): an ML pipeline with a table-segmentation subtask to learn table structure, released with its own annotated training/eval datasets and code (paperswithcode/axcell on GitHub). — [ACL Anthology 2020.emnlp-main.692](https://aclanthology.org/2020.emnlp-main.692/), [arXiv:2004.14356](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.14356), [GitHub](https://github.com/paperswithcode/axcell)
- AxCell's normalization approach is **closed-domain**: "all leaderboards [are] known in advance" (i.e., it links extracted numbers to a fixed taxonomy of (task, dataset, metric) triples rather than open-vocabulary matching), generating textual "evidence" for candidate task/dataset/metric names and applying Naive Bayes inference to match numeric table cells to the correct leaderboard triplet. — [ar5iv:2004.14356](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.14356)
- AxCell resolves synonyms/abbreviations with an abbreviation detector trained on arXiv text (e.g., expanding "en-vi" → "English-Vietnamese"), which the paper reports improved top-1 linking accuracy from 42% to 56% on speech-recognition-related tasks — a concrete, quantified normalization technique. — [ar5iv:2004.14356](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.14356)
- AxCell distinguishes **reported vs. re-implemented/ablation** numbers via a table-type classifier (ULMFiT-based, leaderboard vs. ablation vs. irrelevant, 0.5 confidence threshold), and during extraction/filtering keeps "only the best result" per leaderboard entry, deprioritizing ablation-table numbers relative to principal leaderboard-table numbers. Note: this handles the ablation-vs-main-table distinction but the fetched text did not describe a separate "our re-implementation of X" vs. "reported by X" disambiguation mechanism beyond table typing. — [ar5iv:2004.14356](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.14356)
- AxCell vs. TDMS-IE head-to-head (on the NLP-TDMS LaTeX subset): Task/Dataset/Metric-only F1 61.9 (AxCell) vs. 59.2 (TDMS-IE); full tuple-with-score F1 25.8 (AxCell) vs. 7.5 (TDMS-IE) — roughly a 3.4x improvement in full-tuple extraction. On the larger PWC-Leaderboards set (3,445 leaderboards), AxCell reaches 28.7 micro-F1 for complete (task, dataset, metric, score) extraction. — [ar5iv:2004.14356](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.14356)
- AxCell limitations: requires LaTeX source (available for the large majority, but not all, arXiv papers; PDF-only extraction remains unsolved by this pipeline); results reported only in prose/figures (not tables) are excluded; the closed-domain taxonomy must be manually extended as new leaderboards/tasks emerge; table segmentation is flagged as the pipeline's performance bottleneck. — [ar5iv:2004.14356](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.14356)
- **SciREX** (Jain, Roy, Aggarwal, et al., "SciREX: A Challenge Dataset for Document-Level Information Extraction," ACL 2020): a broader document-level IE benchmark (438 scientific documents, ~5,700 tokens/doc average; 306 train / 66 dev / 66 test after preprocessing) with entity, coreference, and relation annotations; not leaderboard-specific but establishes document-level (as opposed to table-only) extraction as a harder open problem, with a large reported gap between the paper's neural baseline and human performance. — [ACL Anthology 2020.acl-main.670](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.670/), [GitHub allenai/SciREX](https://github.com/allenai/SciREX)
- **Automated Mining of Leaderboards** (Kabongo, D'Souza, Auer, ICADL 2021; arXiv:2109.13089): applies transformer models (BERT, SciBERT, XLNet) to leaderboard-triple extraction and reports the best configuration exceeds 90% F1 for the task, described as new SOTA at the time for leaderboard extraction — a large jump over AxCell/TDMS-IE-era numbers (though likely on an easier/narrower sub-task than full-tuple-with-score extraction; the exact scope of the >90% F1 claim was not independently verified from the primary PDF in this session). — [arXiv:2109.13089](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.13089v1), [Springer/ICADL 2021](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-91669-5_35)
- **ORKG-Leaderboards**: part of the Open Research Knowledge Graph (ORKG) project; described as "a systematic workflow for mining leaderboards as a knowledge graph," extracting task–dataset–metric tuples from AI papers via automated IE combined with human-in-the-loop curation, surfaced through the ORKG front end as structured comparisons and leaderboard plots. ORKG overall indexes 10,000+ papers, 5,000+ research problems across 1,237 fields, 1,000+ comparisons, 224 templates, and 2,216 benchmarks. — [ORKG benchmarks](https://orkg.org/benchmarks), [ResearchGate: ORKG-Leaderboards systematic workflow](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/371604731_ORKG-Leaderboards_a_systematic_workflow_for_mining_leaderboards_as_a_knowledge_graph)
- **LEGOBench** (arXiv:2401.06233, Findings of EMNLP 2024): a benchmark for *evaluating* leaderboard-generation systems, curated from 22 years of arXiv preprint metadata plus 11,000+ PWC leaderboards; defines one language-model-based task config (BM25 retrieves top-10 relevant text chunks, fed to an LM to generate leaderboard entries — "LGPLM") and four graph-based task configs; evaluates encoder-only scientific LMs and decoder-only LLMs; GPT-4 is reported as the best-performing LLM evaluated, but the paper concludes state-of-the-art systems still show "significant performance gaps" on the benchmark — i.e., as of 2024, LLM-based leaderboard generation is not solved. Dataset curation completed June 2023; paper published EMNLP Findings 2024. — [arXiv:2401.06233](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.06233), [ACL Anthology 2024.findings-emnlp.855](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-emnlp.855/)
- **SciLead** (part of "Efficient Performance Tracking: Leveraging Large Language Models for Automated Construction of Scientific Leaderboards," UKPLab, EMNLP 2024 main conference, arXiv:2409.12656): a manually-curated dataset of 27 leaderboards from 43 NLP papers, built specifically to give *complete, accurate* Task-Dataset-Metric-Result annotations directly from publications (addressing incompleteness in earlier auto-derived datasets like PWC-sourced ones). Finding: LLMs are good at identifying task/dataset/metric names and detecting the correct leaderboard, but "struggle with extracting result values" (i.e., the numeric-score extraction step remains the hard part, echoing TDMS-IE's own full-tuple-vs-triple gap from 2019). Llama-3 led on paper coverage; GPT-4 led on result-value coverage/accuracy and correct paper ranking. — [arXiv:2409.12656](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.12656), [GitHub UKPLab/emnlp2024-leaderboard-generation](https://github.com/UKPLab/emnlp2024-leaderboard-generation), [SciLead data (TUdatalib)](https://tudatalib.ulb.tu-darmstadt.de/items/7edc7f83-d6de-4eaa-9eb8-62fe3a30f1b6)
- **League / LAG** ("LLM agents for Leaderboard Auto Generation on Demand" / "League: Leaderboard Generation on Demand," arXiv:2502.18209, 2025): a later (2025) LLM-agent approach to on-demand leaderboard generation; title and existence confirmed via search, but this session did not fetch its full text, so method/result details are a gap (see below). — [arXiv:2502.18209](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.18209)
- **A Position Paper on the Automatic Generation of Machine Learning Leaderboards** (arXiv:2505.17465, 2025): exists as a 2025 position paper on this exact problem area; full content not fetched in this session (gap). — [arXiv:2505.17465](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.17465)
- **MetaLead** ("A Comprehensive Human-Curated Leaderboard Dataset for Transparent Reporting of Machine Learning Experiments," arXiv referenced as 2601.22420 per search index, 2026): a further human-curated leaderboard dataset explicitly framed around "transparent reporting" of ML experiments — suggesting the field is still, as of 2026, treating ground-truth leaderboard data as scarce and worth hand-curating rather than solved by automatic extraction. Full text not fetched in this session (gap); note the arXiv id format (2601.xxxxx) is unusual/should be treated cautiously pending direct verification. — [Lacuna record](https://lacuna.tiptreesystems.com/work/metalead-a-comprehensive-human-curated-leaderboard-dataset-for-transparent/wrk_e4b20eeea4eb6d36aaa12a9e4b80bfb3)
- **Papers with Code shutdown**: PWC — acquired by Facebook/Meta in December 2019 and run under Meta AI Research — was sunset on **July 24, 2025**. At shutdown it hosted 18,000+ papers, 1,500+ leaderboards, and 1,000+ tasks under CC-BY-SA. The domain now redirects to Hugging Face's "Trending Papers" feed (a curated popular-papers list, not a task-by-task SOTA leaderboard replacement); the leaderboards themselves are gone from the live site. Hugging Face's CTO/co-founder Julien Chaumond announced a partnership with Meta around a replacement/successor. Data lives on in a Hugging Face org named `pwc-archive`, described as "the last publicly available snapshot of the PWC datasets." — [Coursera: What Happened to Papers With Code](https://www.coursera.org/articles/papers-with-code), [HyperAI news](https://hyper.ai/en/news/42900), [CenterConsulting AI Library](https://www.centerconsulting.com/ai-library/stories/paperswithcode-shutdown)

### Inferences
- The dominant research trajectory 2019→2024 is a shift from Singh et al.'s **graph/ranking-over-noisy-comparisons** framing (rank papers given an incomplete, noisy tournament) toward **structured tuple extraction** (identify the exact (task, dataset, metric, score) tuple per result) as the primary bottleneck — the field decided that *getting the numbers right* was harder and more valuable than *ranking given noisy numbers*, which is why almost all follow-on systems (TDMS-IE, AxCell, Kabongo et al., LEGOBench, SciLead) report F1 on tuple/entity extraction rather than ranking-quality metrics like the original paper's NDCG/Kendall-tau/Spearman.
- Every one of AxCell, Kabongo et al. 2021, and (implicitly) many later systems depended on the **Papers with Code taxonomy** as a closed-domain gold vocabulary for tasks/datasets/metrics. PWC's July 2025 shutdown is therefore a structural risk for this entire line of work: it was the de facto normalization backbone (the canonical list of "known" task/dataset/metric names that closed-domain linkers matched against), and its removal (with only a static archive snapshot surviving) means future systems either need a new maintained taxonomy (ORKG-Leaderboards is the most visible ongoing candidate) or must fall back to open-vocabulary/LLM-based normalization without a maintained ground truth.
- The persistent, repeatedly-rediscovered finding across TDMS-IE (2019), AxCell (2020), and SciLead (2024) is that **identifying the (task, dataset, metric) triple is comparatively easy; attaching the correct numeric score to it is comparatively hard** (TDMS-IE: 65.3 vs 9.9 macro-F1 triple-vs-full-tuple gap; AxCell: 61.9 vs 25.8; SciLead/2024 LLMs: good at triple ID, weak at result-value extraction). For a system normalizing benchmark results into per-task evidence, this predicts that the highest-value/most fragile step is verifying the *number* is attached to the *right* triple and experimental condition (reported vs. re-implemented, correct split/version), not entity resolution per se.
- No source found in this session directly measures Bradley-Terry/Elo/PageRank-style ranking algorithms against each other on *post-2019* leaderboard data — the ranking-methods comparison question (as opposed to tuple-extraction) appears to have been "settled" by the field around the 2019 Singh et al. result and not revisited empirically in the follow-up leaderboard-extraction literature found here; later work (LEGOBench, SciLead) evaluates LLM generation/extraction quality, not graph-ranking-algorithm quality.

- Full content of "A Position Paper on the Automatic Generation of Machine Learning Leaderboards" (arXiv:2505.17465, 2025) was not fetched — its stance/arguments are unknown beyond the title.
- MetaLead's exact arXiv identifier, authors, and detailed method were not independently verified against a primary source (only a secondary aggregator page, "Lacuna," was found); treat the 2601.22420-style ID and its claims as unverified pending direct arXiv confirmation.

## Normalization problems (synonymous names, splits/versions, metric direction, "our reimplementation" vs. reported) and the techniques used to resolve them

### Takeaway
Across this lineage, three normalization problems recur — (1) open-vocabulary/synonymous dataset and metric names, (2) metric directionality (higher-vs-lower-better) and units/scale, and (3) distinguishing a paper's own reported number from a re-implementation or ablation variant — and the techniques applied progress from cheap heuristics (Singh et al.'s directionality rules and REI-based outlier pruning) to closed-taxonomy matching with abbreviation expansion (AxCell) to table-type classification (AxCell's leaderboard/ablation/irrelevant classifier) to, most recently, LLM-based extraction that still measurably struggles specifically with the numeric-value/result-correctness half of the problem (SciLead 2024).

### Cited Findings
- **Metric-name open vocabulary**: Singh et al. 2019 explicitly flag that "there is no control on the metric names, as they come from an open vocabulary," evidenced by 14,947 unique metric strings extracted from ~99k table citations — no resolved taxonomy is applied in this system; the problem is sidestepped rather than solved (aggregation happens at the paper/edge level, using per-edge metric heuristics for direction only). — [ar5iv:1802.04538](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1802.04538)
- **Metric direction ("higher/lower better")**: Singh et al.'s edge-orientation step applies "simple heuristic rules" such as preferring higher F1/recall/precision scores but lower execution-time values, encoded per-metric rather than inferred automatically — implying the system needs a hand-maintained or heuristic-derived direction table per metric family. — [ar5iv:1802.04538](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1802.04538)
- **Outlier/scale-error detection**: the REI (relative error improvement) filter — dropping edges where (v_z−u_z)/u_z > 100% — is used as a proxy for catching unit/scale mismatches and mis-extractions (e.g., percentage vs. fraction, or misread decimal points), since real improvements almost never exceed 100% relative gain on the same metric (<0.5% of edges in validated leaderboards do). — [ar5iv:1802.04538](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1802.04538)
- **Closed-domain taxonomy + synonym/abbreviation resolution**: AxCell (2020) moves to a closed-domain approach where the full space of (task, dataset, metric) triples is known in advance (from the PWC taxonomy), and resolves surface-form variation via an abbreviation detector (e.g., "en-vi" → "English-Vietnamese"), quantified to raise top-1 linking accuracy from 42% to 56% on a speech-related task family — the most concrete, numerically-validated normalization technique found in this research. — [ar5iv:2004.14356](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.14356)
- **Reported vs. re-implementation / ablation distinction**: AxCell handles this at the table level, not the individual-number level — a ULMFiT classifier labels whole tables as "leaderboard" (principal, comparative results — the kind that should count as a benchmark result for ranking purposes) vs. "ablation" (the paper's own internal variant comparisons, e.g. "w/o attention") vs. "irrelevant" (hyperparameters, dataset stats), and only "the best result" from qualifying tables is kept per leaderboard entry, deprioritizing ablation numbers. This is a table-classification solution, not a fine-grained per-cell "own reported number" vs. "reproduced by a third party" tag — that finer distinction was not found solved anywhere in this lineage. — [ar5iv:2004.14356](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.14356)
- **Splits/versions of datasets**: no source in this session described an explicit technique for disambiguating dataset *version* or *split* (e.g., SQuAD 1.1 vs 2.0, test vs. dev split) as a distinct normalization axis; AxCell's closed-domain taxonomy implicitly requires each split/version to be enumerated as its own taxonomy leaf, but no paper in this research explicitly discussed automated detection/inference of which split a reported number corresponds to. This is a genuine, unresolved gap in the literature surveyed.
- **LLM-era finding on result-value correctness**: SciLead (2024) reports LLMs (GPT-4, Llama-3) are comparatively strong at recognizing task/dataset/metric identity and which leaderboard a result belongs to, but weak specifically at extracting the correct numeric result value — i.e., even with LLM-level language understanding, the "attach the right score, correctly transcribed, to the right triple" step (which subsumes correctly picking among reported/reimplemented/ablation variants in a table) remains the hardest, least-solved part of the normalization pipeline as of late 2024. — [arXiv:2409.12656](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.12656)

### Inferences
- The field's normalization techniques form a rough hierarchy of increasing cost and decreasing generality: (a) cheap universal heuristics (direction-by-metric-name, REI outlier pruning) that scale to open vocabularies but only catch gross errors; (b) closed-taxonomy matching with synonym/abbreviation expansion, which resolves surface-form variance well but requires the taxonomy to be maintained (and is now newly at risk given PWC's shutdown); (c) table/context classification to separate a paper's headline claims from its own internal ablations; (d) LLM-based extraction, which generalizes better to novel task/dataset/metric names than closed taxonomies but is *still* weakest exactly where the earlier heuristic/statistical methods were weakest — getting the attached number right.
- For a system (like StealthLab's procedural-memory substrate) that must normalize heterogeneous benchmark/evidence results into comparable per-task evidence, the literature suggests: treat metric direction and REI-style magnitude sanity-checks as cheap, near-mandatory guardrails regardless of extraction method; do not assume closed-taxonomy matching (Papers with Code-style) will remain externally maintained — PWC itself is now gone; and budget disproportionate verification effort specifically on the "is this the right number, correctly attributed (not an ablation, not someone else's reproduction, not a stale split)" step, since every method surveyed — heuristic, transformer-classifier, and LLM — treats this as the hardest sub-problem.

### Gaps
- No primary source found describing an automated, general technique for dataset-version/split disambiguation (e.g., automatically detecting "test" vs "dev," or dataset version numbers, from table/caption text) — this appears to be handled only implicitly via closed-taxonomy enumeration, not solved as a distinct NLP problem in any surveyed paper.
- No primary source found with a fine-grained (per-cell, not per-table) mechanism for tagging "this number is the original authors' own reported result" vs. "this number is a third party's re-implementation of a baseline it's comparing against" — AxCell's ablation/leaderboard table classifier is the closest analog but operates at table granularity.

## Ranking from noisy, sparse pairwise comparisons: Bradley-Terry/Plackett-Luce, Elo/TrueSkill, Rank Centrality, spectral methods, robustness, and applicability

### Takeaway
Rank Centrality (Negahban, Oh & Shah, *Operations Research* 65(1), 2016/2017) is the canonical spectral/random-walk method for aggregating sparse pairwise comparisons into a global ranking, provably matching the Bradley-Terry-Luce maximum-likelihood estimator's performance on BTL-generated data while being computationally cheaper and more robust under sparsity; more recent work (2020–2025) has extended robustness analysis to extremely sparse and adversarial comparison networks and shown Bradley-Terry/Plackett-Luce rankings are notably more robust to missing data than naive score-averaging — directly relevant to ranking models/procedures from partial, noisy evidence.

### Cited Findings
- **Rank Centrality** (Negahban, Oh, Shah, "Rank Centrality: Ranking from Pairwise Comparisons," *Operations Research*, Vol. 65, No. 1, pp. 266–287, published online 2016 / print 2017): an iterative rank-aggregation algorithm with "a natural random walk interpretation over the graph of objects where an edge is present between a pair of objects if they are compared"; an object's Rank Centrality score is its stationary probability under this random walk. — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Rank-Centrality:-Ranking-from-Pairwise-Comparisons-Negahban-Oh/ad8d6d835be4663cd179c1605456a0bcd71f4188), [INFORMS/Operations Research](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/opre.2016.1534)
- On synthetic data generated from the Bradley-Terry-Luce (BTL) model, Rank Centrality "performs as well as the maximum likelihood estimator for that model and outperforms other popular ranking algorithms" — i.e., it is asymptotically competitive with the BTL-MLE (effectively Bradley-Terry/Elo-family estimation) while being derived from a spectral/graph-centrality method rather than direct likelihood maximization. — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Rank-Centrality:-Ranking-from-Pairwise-Comparisons-Negahban-Oh/ad8d6d835be4663cd179c1605456a0bcd71f4188)
- Rank Centrality's stated application domains include ranking online gamers, chess players, aggregating social opinions, and deciding which products to sell based on transaction (implicit-comparison) data — general-purpose sparse-pairwise-comparison ranking, not domain-specific. — [Illinois Experts profile](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/rank-centrality-ranking-from-pairwise-comparisons/)
- **Sparse-network extensions**: Han, Xu & Chen, "A General Pairwise Comparison Model for Extremely Sparse Networks" (JASA, work dated ~2020/2023 per search results), directly targets ranking robustness/consistency when the comparison graph is extremely sparse — a generalization of the classic Bradley-Terry sparse-asymptotics line (Han et al. 2020, *Annals of Applied Probability*, "asymptotic theory of sparse Bradley-Terry model"). — [arXiv:2002.08853](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.08853)
- **Robustness comparison, BT/Plackett-Luce vs. naive aggregation**: search-indexed summary of recent work states "Plackett-Luce often achieves the highest correlation, particularly for sparse datasets and coverage metrics," and that "BT and Plackett-Luce rankings are markedly more robust to missing data than mean- or sum-based metric aggregation" — i.e., model-based pairwise-comparison ranking outperforms naive score-averaging specifically under sparsity/missingness, which is the exact regime a partial-evidence procedural-memory system would face. A "triplet-ratio measure" is cited as capturing ranking consistency even when many per-dataset evaluations are absent. (This finding came from a search-engine synthesis across several 2025-ish arXiv papers rather than one directly fetched primary source — treat as moderately, not fully, verified.) — [search synthesis, primary sources listed below]
- Related/underlying primary sources for the above robustness claim (titles/dates only, not individually fetched in full): "Bradley-Terry Rankings for Recommender Systems Across Dataset Taxonomies" (arXiv:2606.07492); "Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons" (arXiv:2501.02505; also published in *Communications Physics*); "Recent advances in the Bradley-Terry Model: theory, algorithms, and applications" (arXiv:2601.14727); "Isotonic Bradley-Terry Model for Paired Comparison Data" (arXiv:2608.02081). — [arXiv:2606.07492](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.07492v1), [arXiv:2501.02505 / Nature Comms Physics](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42005-025-02461-y), [arXiv:2601.14727](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.14727), [arXiv:2608.02081](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.02081)
- **Partial-ranking-under-uncertainty framing**: "Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons" (*Communications Physics*, arXiv:2501.02505) argues that "in situations with limited and/or noisy comparisons, it is often challenging to confidently distinguish item performance based on evidence available," and that most ranking methods "force a complete ordering suggesting a meaningful distinction when there is none" — motivating a Bayesian framework that outputs *partial* rankings (explicitly grouping items that cannot be confidently distinguished) rather than always forcing a strict total order. — [Nature Communications Physics](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42005-025-02461-y)
- **Plackett-Luce flexibility**: the Plackett-Luce model natively accommodates complete rankings, partial rankings, incomplete rankings, pairwise comparisons, and groupwise/listwise comparisons within one formulation — relevant because real benchmark-evidence data arrives in exactly this mix of forms (sometimes a full leaderboard order, sometimes just one pairwise "we beat X" claim). — [search synthesis of Plackett-Luce inference literature, e.g. Maystre & Grover NeurIPS 2015 fast PL inference](http://papers.neurips.cc/paper/5681-fast-and-accurate-inference-of-plackettluce-models.pdf)
- No direct primary-source coverage of Elo or TrueSkill specifically (as distinct from Bradley-Terry, of which Elo is a sequential/online special case) was retrieved in this session beyond their being named in search-tool summaries; TrueSkill's Gaussian/Bayesian belief-propagation properties were not independently verified here. This is a gap.

### Inferences
- The strongest, best-evidenced statistical lesson transferable to a "rank models/procedures from partial evidence" system is Negahban/Oh/Shah's core result: a spectral/random-walk aggregator (Rank Centrality) matches BTL maximum-likelihood performance while being cheaper and — critically — was independently shown by Singh et al. 2019 (a PageRank variant, which is architecturally a close cousin of Rank Centrality's random-walk formulation) to *outperform* tournament/MLE-style methods specifically on real, sparse, disconnected scientific-comparison graphs. This convergence (two independent lines of work, one theoretical/synthetic and one empirical/real-world, both favoring random-walk/spectral aggregation over direct tournament MLE under sparsity) is a meaningful, citable double-confirmation for preferring PageRank/Rank-Centrality-style diffusion methods over Elo/Bradley-Terry-direct-MLE when the underlying evidence graph is expected to be sparse and disconnected — as benchmark-comparison evidence typically is.
- The 2025-era "partial rankings" line (arXiv:2501.02505) offers a design principle directly applicable to a procedural-memory system: rather than forcing every procedure/model into a strict total order from noisy, sparse evidence, the system should be willing to output *ties* or *indistinguishable groups* when the evidence doesn't support a confident distinction — mirroring Singh et al.'s own finding that disconnected/sparse regions of the comparison graph (MIT-300) produced much less reliable rankings (Spearman 0.23–0.53) than well-connected ones (SQuAD, 0.89–0.94).

### Gaps
- No primary-source-verified, apples-to-apples empirical comparison of Rank Centrality specifically (as opposed to generic PageRank) against Bradley-Terry-MLE *on real scientific-leaderboard data* (as opposed to synthetic BTL data) was found in this session — the Negahban et al. result is synthetic-data-based; the Singh et al. real-data result used PageRank, not Rank Centrality by name, so the two are related but not identical, and no source directly bridges them.
- TrueSkill (Microsoft's Bayesian skill-rating system) was not covered by any primary source fetched in this session; its robustness properties under sparse/noisy comparisons relative to Bradley-Terry/Rank-Centrality remain an open gap for this research.
- The "Plackett-Luce often achieves highest correlation... markedly more robust to missing data than mean/sum aggregation" claim rests on a search-engine synthesis rather than a directly fetched and read primary paper; the specific paper(s) making this exact comparison were not identified with full confidence (several 2025-dated arXiv IDs were surfaced but not individually confirmed as the source of this specific claim).
